Title: A Desk Is Not Yet a Mission
Date: 2026-10-19
Category: The liaison desk
Deck: On arriving, listening, and resisting the urge to become another source of reporting requirements.

On my first full day in the headquarters, I established the location of my workspace. This is a modest sentence, but every new staff officer knows the relief contained in it. There is now a place to put the notebook. The notebook, in turn, provides somewhere to write down all the things one does not yet understand.

An orientation followed. I met people across the staff, learned something about their responsibilities, and began absorbing the local geography of expertise. A headquarters has a formal structure, and then it has the structure you discover by asking a question and hearing, three times in succession, that you really need to speak to someone else. The third person is often extremely helpful.

My job is liaison. In practical terms, that means helping two parts of a large organization understand what each needs from the other before a misunderstanding acquires a deadline and becomes everyone's emergency.

It sounds straightforward until you try to explain what a productive day looks like. A useful conversation may prevent a problem nobody will ever see. An unhelpful conversation may generate a magnificent email chain. The email chain is easier to count.

I began working into the meeting schedule. The Army calls this a battle rhythm, a phrase that grants considerable dignity to a calendar. The real question is where my presence can change something: surface a dependency, get a question answered, or carry an accurate decision back to the people who need it. Occupying a chair is only the opening move.

In my update to the command, I asked for expectations and boundaries. What should I handle independently? What should I elevate? What information is useful, and how often? These are less glamorous questions than announcing a new initiative. They also reduce the likelihood that my new initiative will be a reporting requirement imposed on someone who already has eleven.

I have been on both sides of that transaction. There is a dangerous moment when a person trying to understand an organization decides that everybody else should fill out a template. The template feels like progress because its columns are aligned. The cost appears later, in the calendars of people who were previously doing the work.

The product lesson is familiar: start with the problem and the people experiencing it. Understand the constraints. Agree on the outcome. Give the person responsible enough room to exercise judgment. None of this requires a kickoff deck, although a kickoff deck may appear anyway, drawn by the disturbance.

The first day ended with a better map, some useful introductions, and more specific questions. I can live with that. I have a desk. The mission will be measured by what moves through it.
