Title: Reporting for Paperwork
Date: 2026-10-09
Category: Field notes
Deck: The first task of joining an institution is persuading its constituent parts that you have joined it.

There is a particular optimism available only to a man who has arrived in a new assignment and has not yet discovered how many separate offices must agree that he exists.

I brought that optimism to Germany. I had orders, a job, and a sincere desire to be useful. These seemed, briefly, like the essential ingredients. An institution of sufficient size can take those three items and produce a fourth: an appointment.

The early days involved the familiar business of finding a place to live, submitting paperwork, and learning which sequence of perfectly reasonable requirements would permit the next perfectly reasonable requirement to begin. From a distance, this resembles administration. Up close, it is a small logistics campaign in which the principal item of supply is yourself.

The people helping me were excellent. This is an important detail, and it complicates the usual story about bureaucracy. The satisfying version has an incompetent official guarding a stamp. The more common version has several competent people, each holding one part of a process that nobody can see from end to end. Everyone is helping. You are still carrying a folder.

My sponsor supplied the kind of context an organizational chart cannot: who understands what, which conversations matter, and where a newcomer can contribute without first establishing an independent republic of unsolicited advice. Other people helped me navigate the administrative requirements. Their generosity made the system navigable. It also made clear how much of the system depended on generosity.

I sent an arrival update. I was getting settled. I was grateful for the help. I wanted to start contributing. I asked for a conversation about priorities.

That last part deserves more attention than it usually gets. A new person can spend a remarkable amount of energy demonstrating eagerness before discovering what anyone actually needs. Eagerness is abundant. A shared understanding of the job is scarcer and has fewer opportunities for ceremonial display.

As a product manager, I recognize the temptation. You arrive, you see friction, and somewhere in the back of your head a feature begins putting on its shoes. Perhaps a dashboard. Perhaps automation. Perhaps a dashboard that automates another dashboard. Meanwhile, the person doing the work needs to know who has the request and what happens next.

I am trying to let that person finish the sentence before I design anything.

For now, the useful work is modest: establish the relationships, understand the expectations, and make the next handoff a little less mysterious. A folder is not a product strategy. But carrying one around long enough can improve your questions.
