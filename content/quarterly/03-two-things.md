Title: Two Things, Both of Them Separate
Date: 2026-10-21
Category: The liaison desk
Deck: An appreciation of the surprisingly consequential act of knowing which thing everyone is talking about.

A substantial portion of my day was spent establishing that two things were, in fact, two things.

They occupied the same broad neighborhood of staff attention. They attracted overlapping vocabulary. They involved enough of the same institutional furniture that a person moving quickly could mistake proximity for identity. I was that person, or at least close enough to him that the correction belongs in the first person.

The remedy was not intellectually glamorous. Separate source material. Separate owners. Separate questions. A clear understanding of which conversation belonged to which effort. Nobody awards a ribbon for keeping two folders apart, but the absence of such a ribbon should not be taken as evidence that the work lacks value.

An error of this kind acquires strength through circulation. Put the wrong relationship into a crisp summary and the crispness becomes part of its authority. Forward it twice and someone may start planning around it. By the time the distinction is recovered, the error has introduced itself to several senior people and developed a position on the agenda.

I spent time finding the people who could explain the work firsthand and obtaining the products that actually described it. That second step matters. A document can be available without being current. It can be current without being approved. It can be approved and still answer a different question from the one on your desk.

The oldest draft is often the easiest file to find. It has had more time to make friends.

One of the useful outcomes was a sharper sense of where I could help: identify the external dependencies, put an owner against the next action, and keep distinct efforts distinct. The liaison role starts looking less like a relay station and more like a persistent effort to preserve meaning while information travels.

This also happens in product work. Two customers describe something using the same phrase, and a team concludes that it has found one requirement. One person needs visibility. The other needs authority. Build a single solution around the shared noun and you can disappoint both with impressive efficiency.

The answer is to stay with the specifics long enough to understand them. Who needs what? What decision will it support? Which source establishes the claim? What remains uncertain? These questions sound slow until you compare them with the speed of confidently coordinating the wrong thing.

By the end of the day, the picture was less tidy in the superficial sense and more useful in the practical one. There were two efforts, with their own people and next steps. The correction did not create a new capability. It made the existing work easier to do accurately.

Some days, professional progress consists of removing the conjunction between two nouns. I recommend recording it anyway.
