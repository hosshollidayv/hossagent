Title: The Document Was Legible, but the Scanner Was Not Satisfied
Date: 2026-10-26
Category: Systems & people
Deck: Two copies of one document become a small lesson in what a requirement actually means.

The first copy had already been rejected because it was difficult to read. I did not want to send it again.

This seemed like an uncomplicated editorial position. A better copy existed. The task was to make that copy small enough to transmit while preserving the very quality that made it worth sending: legibility.

Yet the problem had a way of sliding back toward the old file. The old file was present. It was familiar. It was available to be attached. These are powerful attributes in an administrative environment, even when the file's most relevant attribute is that it has already failed.

I wanted the actual document examined. Could the recipient read it? Would the version we sent answer the request? Could we reduce the size without returning to the condition that prompted the rejection?

The person helping process the paperwork was an intermediary, not the villain of the story. That distinction matters. Administrative frustration often looks for a human face because the process itself is difficult to address directly. The nearest helpful person should not become the target simply because he answered the email.

The better response was to anticipate what was needed and send a usable document with a short message. A cleaner explanation alone would not fix an unreadable attachment. A new attachment alone would not help if it reproduced the defect.

There is a whole category of work hiding in that distinction: formally supplying the requested thing versus supplying something the next person can actually use.

A file can exist, open, travel, and still fail its purpose. The purpose here was wonderfully concrete. Someone needed to read the page.

Before the next salvo, I wanted us to look at the damn page.
